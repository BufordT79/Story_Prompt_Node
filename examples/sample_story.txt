The Ferryman's Key

Mira reached the rope bridge just as the sun broke over the gorge. Far below, the river ran black and fast, and the planks swayed under her boots with every step. She kept one hand on the frayed guide rope and the other pressed to her chest, where the brass key hung on a cord beneath her jacket — the key her grandmother had mailed her with no letter, no explanation, only a river stone wrapped in newspaper.

On the far bank, Jonas was waiting with his flat-bottomed ferry, a lantern burning at the bow even in daylight. He said nothing as she climbed aboard, only pushed off with his long pole and let the fog swallow the banks. The mist moved in slow ribbons over the water, and somewhere ahead a wheel was creaking, vast and patient, like a clock that had never once stopped.

The Old Mill rose out of the fog all at once — three stories of crumbling stone, its moss-covered wheel turning in the current, windows dark as sockets. Storm clouds were stacking up behind the ridge, and the first cold wind slapped the river into whitecaps as Jonas tied off at the landing. "Your grandmother kept the light on forty years," he said. "Somebody has to."

Inside, the mill was a cathedral of dust. Shafts of gray light fell through gaps in the roof and lit the great wooden gears, still and furred with cobwebs, each tooth as long as Mira's forearm. She crossed the floor slowly, boots printing the dust, until she found it behind the grindstone: a low iron door, no handle, only a brass keyhole polished bright by decades of use.

The key turned with a sound like a struck bell. Cold blue light spilled through the opening seam, washing over her face and throwing her shadow twenty feet up the stone wall.

The stair beneath led down to a long vaulted cellar, and Mira stopped on the last step with her breath gone. Hundreds of glass jars lined the shelves, floor to ceiling, each one glowing softly from inside — river-light, her grandmother had called it in the one story she ever told. The jars pulsed gently, all together, like a field of drowsy fireflies, and their glow turned the stone vault the color of deep water.

She was still standing there when the lantern light came down the stairs behind her. Jonas held it high, and the flame revealed what the jars' glow had hidden: the entire back wall was a painted mural — the river, the mill, the bridge, and a girl with a key, painted decades before Mira was born.

By morning the storm had passed. Mira stood at the mill door as the sun came up, and for the first time in forty years the wheel turned for a new keeper. The river ran gold in the early light, and on the water Jonas raised one hand from his ferry, a small dark shape gliding home through the shining current.
